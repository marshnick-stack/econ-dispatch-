A Guide for International School Staff

Student Data & China's Privacy Laws

What the law says, how to check your own school, and what to do about what you find

This is a plain-English guide to how China's data protection laws apply to the ordinary work of teaching — marking, sharing files, asking students to sign up to a website, building your own tools. It is written for teachers and school leaders, not lawyers.

Before you read any further

This guide is for staff awareness. It is not legal advice. It sets out how the law is generally understood and how to check your own situation — but for any formal decision, your school should take advice from someone qualified in Chinese data protection law.

Nothing here is an assessment of any particular school. Where it describes a common finding, it is describing a pattern across international schools in China, and giving you the means to check whether it applies to yours.

1 · The Four Laws That Matter

Why this applies to schools, and not only to tech companies

China has strict national law about how personal data — including student data — is collected, stored and moved. Since 2021 it applies fully to international schools operating in the mainland. The gap between common practice and legal requirement is one most international schools share, so this is not about blame. It is about making informed decisions rather than uninformed ones.

PIPL — Personal Information Protection Law

China's principal data privacy law, broadly comparable to the EU's GDPR. In force since November 2021. Sets the rules for collecting, storing and transferring personal data.

CSL — Cybersecurity Law

The foundational network security framework, in force since 2017. Requires important data collected in China to be stored in China.

Law on Protection of Minors

Extra protection for anyone under 18. Processing a minor's data requires explicit consent from a parent or guardian — not from the student.

Interim Measures for Generative AI (2023)

Covers AI services operating in China. Directly relevant whenever staff put student work through an AI tool.

What PIPL actually requires

  • Lawful basis — a legal reason to process the data. For schools, usually consent.
  • Informed, explicit consent — for minors, written parental consent. Not implied, not a pre-ticked box.
  • Purpose limitation — use it only for what you collected it for.
  • Data minimisation — collect only what is strictly necessary.
  • Data localisation — if the person is in China, their data stays in China unless a formal transfer mechanism exists.
  • Records — keep processing records for at least three years.
  • Breach notification — notify affected individuals and the regulator within a reasonable time.

The regulator is the Cyberspace Administration of China (CAC). Enforcement has so far concentrated on large technology platforms rather than schools — but the obligation exists regardless of who is currently being pursued, and enforcement priorities move. Penalties run to ¥50 million or 5% of annual turnover, with personal liability possible in serious cases.

2 · What Counts as Personal Data

More than most people assume

Under PIPL, personal information is anything that can identify a specific individual — alone, or combined with other information you hold. In a school that is a wide category.

Treated as sensitive, requiring the highest protection

Grades and assessment results · health and medical records · disciplinary records · and any data belonging to a student under 18, which is automatically classed as sensitive in its entirety.

Standard personal information includes full names, school email addresses, student ID numbers, class or group IDs, and parent contact details. All of it is covered, however harmless it looks.

The initials misconception

Using initials instead of full names does not change the legal position. In a classroom, where you can identify which student "W.Z." is, initials are still personal information. The test is whether a reasonable person in your position could identify the individual — not whether a stranger could.

The golden rule. Any processing of personal information belonging to people located in China must happen on infrastructure physically located in mainland China, unless a specific, state-approved cross-border transfer mechanism is in place.

3 · Cross-Border Transfers

The single largest source of non-compliance in international schools

Any time personal data belonging to someone in China reaches a server, company or individual outside the mainland, that is a cross-border transfer — including when it merely routes through Hong Kong. That covers saving a student's name to a database hosted abroad, uploading student work to a foreign AI, students creating accounts on foreign websites, and any app syncing to a foreign cloud service.

To do it lawfully a school generally needs specific parental consent, a documented impact assessment, and often a CAC-approved standard contract with the foreign recipient. Parental consent on its own is not sufficient — that catches many schools out.

4 · Checking Your Own School

The first thing to look at, and it takes ten seconds

For most international schools in China the largest single exposure is not an EdTech platform. It is the office suite — because it touches every teacher and every student, not one class or one department.

Microsoft 365 comes in two forms. The global version stores files in Hong Kong, Singapore, Japan or the United States — outside mainland China, and therefore non-compliant without a transfer mechanism. The version operated by 21Vianet, a licensed Chinese partner, stores data in Beijing and Shanghai and is compliant. Most international schools are on the global version, because it was easier to set up, cheaper, and deployed before PIPL existed.

How to tell which one you are on

  1. Check the login URL. Sign in and look at the address bar. login.microsoftonline.com means your data is outside China. login.partner.microsoftonline.cn means it is inside.
  2. Check OneDrive settings → Options → look for Data location or Region. "China" or "21Vianet" is inside; "Asia Pacific", "Hong Kong", "Singapore", "US" or "Global" is outside.
  3. Check the desktop app. "OneDrive for Business" is the global version; "OneDrive for Business operated by 21Vianet" is the China one.
  4. Ask IT directly: "Is our Microsoft 365 tenant the global version or the 21Vianet-operated China version?" If they are not sure, that uncertainty is itself the finding.

If it turns out to be the global version, then every spreadsheet of grades, every document with a student name, every Teams chat and every SharePoint folder is sitting outside China. The point of checking is not to produce alarm — it is that a school cannot make a decision about a risk it has not measured. The available responses are to migrate to 21Vianet, to adopt China-hosted document platforms such as Tencent Docs or Alibaba Docs for anything containing student data, or to formally and knowingly accept the risk in writing. All three are legitimate. Not knowing is not.

A worked example of doing this properly

ManageBac is a case where the question has a documented answer. Faria have confirmed that school environments on the .cn domain are hosted on servers within China, under ICP licence 17051512, with an Information Classified Security Protection Certificate. Their security documentation is published at faria.org/secure.

Two caveats worth copying as a method. This applies only to the .cn domain — a school on the global domain is in a different position. And you should ask for a Data Processing Agreement in writing and keep it. That is what a verified vendor looks like: a named legal entity, a licence number, a written confirmation, and a document in your file.

5 · Four Things People Get Wrong

Each of these is a common and confident mistake

"We route through Hong Kong, so we're fine"

Under Article 38 what matters is the final destination of the data, not the route it takes. A Hong Kong proxy is a connectivity workaround, not a legal safeguard.

"It's a .cn domain, so the data is in China"

A .cn address is a web address. A foreign company can use one for a CDN node or a localised login page while its databases stay abroad. To be satisfied, ask three questions: are the core databases physically in the mainland, is a licensed Chinese entity the data controller, and is there a PIPL-compliant Data Processing Agreement? Without documented answers to all three, assume not.

"I did it on my own laptop, on my own VPN"

If the work is school work, the school's obligations follow it. Using a personal VPN to put student work through a foreign AI service on a personal device is still processing student data through a foreign service. The device and the network are not the deciding factor.

"We were audited a few years ago and we were fine"

Any audit predating November 2021 reflects the law before PIPL came into force. PIPL changed the requirements substantially. An older audit provides no ongoing cover.

6 · AI, Marking and Feedback

Where this touches teaching most directly

The rule here is simple. Putting student work through a foreign AI service is a cross-border transfer. ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini and Copilot all run on servers outside China, and reaching them through a VPN does not change that. There is no data processing agreement, no parental consent for that specific transfer, and generally no way to have the data deleted afterwards.

Domestic services — Baidu Wenxin Yiyan, Alibaba Tongyi Qianwen, Tencent Hunyuan, Moonshot's Kimi, DeepSeek and other CAC-registered providers — keep the processing inside the mainland, so no transfer occurs. A useful shorthand: if the API endpoint is a .cn domain and the service expects Chinese ID verification and payment in CNY, it is operating inside the domestic framework.

The part most people miss, in their favour

Using a foreign AI tool to write lesson plans, worksheets or teaching materials — with no student data in the prompt — raises no PIPL issue at all. The obligation attaches to the student's personal information, not to the tool.

So the practical line is not "foreign AI is banned". It is: foreign models for your own work, domestic models the moment a student's name, work or grade is involved.

One further distinction worth knowing: an enterprise API plan from a domestic provider usually carries data protection terms that the consumer chatbot does not. For anything school-wide, the API route is the one to take.

7 · EdTech Websites and Student Sign-Ups

When you direct students to a platform, the data flow becomes the school's responsibility

A student "choosing" to sign up does not shift the obligation. For each platform students are directed to use, the school is expected to carry out an impact assessment beforehand, tell parents what the platform is and where the data goes, obtain separate explicit consent for the transfer, have a lawful transfer mechanism in place, and record the platform in a data register.

School email addresses are personal information, so a student signing up with one is itself a transfer. Single sign-on is no different — it passes the student's unique identifier to the third party.

"But every international school uses these tools"

True, and it does not change the legal position. Widespread non-compliance is not permission, and regulators have a habit of tolerating a grey area and then making an example of one case.

The realistic path is not to switch everything off tomorrow. It is to start a documented process: audit what is already in use, get proper consent for the tools that matter most, and review anything new before students are pointed at it.

Two things that genuinely reduce the risk

  • Student-initiated use. If a student finds and uses a site entirely on their own, the school's exposure is much lower. The question is whether the school created the context in which they encountered it. If you linked it, set it, or built it into the course, you own it.
  • Username-only sign-up. A self-chosen username with no real name or school email is materially lower risk — but "no email required" is not the same as "no personal data collected". Plenty of free platforms monetise behavioural data. Read the privacy policy first.

The single most useful thing to build: a vendor list

One document recording every platform students are asked to use, its country of registration, what data is shared, and whether consent has been obtained. Most schools have no such list, which means no one can answer basic questions about their own exposure. It is low effort and it makes every subsequent decision possible.

Leadership may decide to accept the risk of a given platform. That is a legitimate strategic choice. But it has to be a decision that is made, recorded, justified and revisited — not a decision that happens by default because nobody looked.

8 · Tools You Build Yourself

Higher obligations, because you become the data controller

Teachers increasingly build their own tools — gradebooks, browser extensions, tracking apps. These carry every obligation a purchased platform does, and arguably more, because the school and the person who built it become responsible for the data.

A real example, and it was mine

A gradebook app I built was syncing student first names, class IDs and initials to a Supabase database. Supabase is hosted outside China. Even that minimal data — enough to identify specific students in context — made it an unlawful cross-border transfer. The app came offline once I realised.

The lesson is not "don't build things". It is that the compliance question belongs at the architecture stage, when it costs nothing, rather than after you have built something you then have to switch off.

What a compliant build looks like

  1. Keep the data local or domestic. A local SQLite database on the device, or a China-region cloud database. If devices need to sync, peer-to-peer between school machines avoids a foreign server entirely.
  2. Get specific, documented parental consent. A general IT policy does not cover it. Name the app, say what it collects and why, say where it is stored, and use an active opt-in.
  3. Do an impact assessment before it touches student data. It does not have to be long. It has to exist.
  4. Keep a three-year log of access and changes. A table in the same local database is enough.
  5. Avoid foreign backends. Supabase, Firebase and non-China AWS regions are all outside the mainland.

No-code builders are the quiet trap

Glide, Bubble, Adalo, AppSheet and similar are almost always hosted in the US or EU. Building a student-data app on one is non-compliant unless it offers a China-hosted option. Check where the data lives before you put a single student on it — the convenience of no-code does not change the transfer rules.

9 · Where to Start

Ordered by effort against urgency

This week — low effort, high value

  • Check which Microsoft 365 tenant your school is on. Ten seconds, and it tells you the size of the largest question.
  • Take any staff-built tool that syncs student data to a foreign server offline, or disable the sync.
  • Pause new student sign-ups to foreign platforms until there is a way to review them.
  • Agree who owns this. A named person, not a committee.
  • Ask staff to stop putting student work through foreign AI services until there is an approved list to point them at.

This term — needs coordination

  • Build the vendor list: every platform, its country, the data shared, the consent status.
  • Draft a parental consent form and privacy notice for the platforms that matter most.
  • Publish a digital tools page for parents, naming the core platforms and linking their privacy policies.
  • Issue an approved list of domestic AI tools, so "don't use that one" comes with "use this instead".
  • Look at whether enrolment agreements say anything about cross-border use of EdTech.
  • If the office suite is the global version, cost out the alternatives before the decision is needed.

Ongoing

  • A review step before any new platform requiring student login is adopted.
  • Impact assessments for the major processing activities.
  • Three-year processing logs on the key systems.
  • Staff-built tools held to the same architecture standard as purchased ones.
The goal is not to stop using technology. It is to use it in a way that is transparent with parents, keeps student data where the law requires it, and protects both the school and the individual teacher from a risk nobody chose.
